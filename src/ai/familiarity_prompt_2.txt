TASK
Rate each given word or phrase for availability/familiarity in English.

Input words and phrases will come along with a classification, which will be one of the following:
Concept, Collocation, Formula, Non-unit, Nonsense
Don't read into the given classifications, but do follow Core Rules referring to them.

CORE RULES (Run through these mentally for EVERY item before outputting)
- US Audience Perspective: Assume a general audience of well-rounded educated adult native speakers without any particular expert knowledge.
- Real Usage over Formal Rules: Score based on actual, real-world usage (including common nonstandard slang/idioms), not formal dictionary rules.
- Tie-Breaker: On borderline cases, default to the higher numbered tier (e.g., choosing Tier 4 over Tier 3).
- For the Spoken Tiers, do not score based on how easy a phrase is to understand. 
  The Coffee Shop Test: Imagine overhearing 100 hours of casual, unstructured chatter in a local coffee shop. If the probability of hearing the 
  EXACT string spoken naturally is close to zero, it must go to a Written Tier (typically WT1 or WT2), no matter how simple or transparent the meaning is.
  Only items classified as Concept or Formula can be rated in these tiers.
- For the Written Tiers, score on how easily the phrase is to understand, not how often it appears.
  Any classification can be rated here, but items classified as Collocation must be rated in these tiers.

RUBRIC
------
SPOKEN TIERS - Active / Conversational Vocabulary

S1 — Beginner Core
The fundamental building blocks of English: words and phrases typical of early beginner instruction. 
It's extremely difficult to achieve this level.
Examples: hello, water, thank you, good morning, OK, please, how are you

S2 — Everyday Active Vocabulary
Seen or heard every day. The ubiquitous terms of everyday life. Feels fundamental to a native speaker. 
- The Dominance Rule: If a phrase has a more common, direct synonym that a native speaker would default to 80% of the time 
  (e.g., "gone crazy" vs. "gone nuts"), the less common synonym cannot be ST2.
- The 24-Hour Reality Check: To qualify for S2, a word or phrase must be something the target adult has personally spoken or heard out loud 
  in the last 24 hours.
Examples: schedule, neighbor, make sense, hang out, Starbucks, Disney, TV, deal with

S3 — Infrequent Active Vocabulary (Simple but Situational)
Universally known and used effortlessly without thought, but highly situational. 
These are words a 10-year-old knows perfectly, but doesn't need to use unless a specific event triggers them.
Examples: gut feeling, blackout, Shakespeare, Tiger Woods, RSVP, learning curve, coincidence 

S4 — Particular Active Vocabulary
Universally recognized, but not produced conversationally by all. 
Some people like to say it, to others it never occurs to them. Might be various ways to say the same thing.
Examples: devil's advocate, apocalypse, the elephant in the room, a double-edged sword, smoke and mirrors, ego, guru, cliché, micromanage

WRITTEN TIERS - Items seen mainly in written material

W1 — Transparent Meaning
Universally understood by target adult readers.
Includes easy collocations and common concepts and phrases. Doesn't include many single words because qualifying ones would likely be in Spoken Tiers.
Examples: aquatic animal, self-organizing, prior success, foreign national

W2 — Good Understanding
The target adult understands it, even though the meaning isn't completely transparent. 
Feels like easy general knowledge trivia.
Examples: retroactive, paradox, synthesize, caveat, vicious cycle, cognitive dissonance

W3 — Partial Understanding
The target adult understands it only in context. 
Attentive readers will look it up to ensure correct interpretation.
Examples: Sisyphean, a Faustian bargain, anachronism, obfuscate, Schrödinger's cat

W4 — Vague Recognition
The target adult has seen it before, but the definition is insecure even in context.
It rings a bell. They would need to look it up to have any confidence.
Examples: anathema, loquacious, ad hominem, deus ex machina, Machiavellian, mercurial, et al.

W5 — Niche Only
A small specialized segment knows it; the target adult might never have seen it. 
Not encountered in ordinary reading without explanation.
Examples: catachresis, anagnorisis, terminus ante quem, sub specie aeternitatis, Frege, op. cit., sine qua non, epistemic, Derrida

W6 — Near-Nonexistent
Virtually nobody knows it; may surface only in obscure sources. Barely present in the language.
Examples: nudiustertian, gongoozler, lucus a non lucendo, ignotum per ignotius, Chrysippus, Philolaus, sc., sqq.

OTHER TIERS

Uninterpretable
Was not able to interpret the exact provided string.
This could be due to a misspelling, bad grammar, or the inability to extract any meaning.

OUTPUT
At the end of the output, present each final evaluation in the following strict format, with no introductory or auxiliary text:

<original word/phrase> : <tier>

Example outputs:
thank you : S1
Franz Kafka : W3
nudiustertian : W6
candy once flown : Uninterpretable
