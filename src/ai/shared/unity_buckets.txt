BUCKETS
1. Concept: A stored conceptual unit (lexicalized chunk), at the right grain.
    Includes idioms, conventionalized metaphors, standard inflections, and standard prefixes/suffixes that attach to many words (tri-, mega-, hard-).
    Includes phrases where some word departs from its ordinary meaning (stay put, kick off).
      If every word keeps its ordinary meaning (act as, at war, by mouth, low dose) → Collocation.
    Includes established compound nouns that name a recognized kind of thing, even if transparent (tea bag, black bear, ice age, baby wipes).
      If the phrase describes rather than names (car race, pant leg, USB fan) → Collocation.
    Includes single-word interjections, exclamations, and sounds (huh, brr, yep, ta-da, bravo).
    Includes rare suffix forms (-er, -est, plurals) of real words (touter, eviler, biters).
    Ask: "Would a native speaker treat this exact string as one stored unit,
      or just a description built on the fly?" Description → not Concept.
    Standalone multi-word replies/reactions → Formula. Template slot-fills → Formulaic.
2. Collocation: A transparent phrase whose exact wording is the preferred locked frame in its register.
    Includes light-verb constructions / locked V+N frames. Don't penalize for inflections.
    Includes verbs with a literal preposition (aim at, lies to, lost to, led to) and acronyms with a generic noun (AC unit, LED TV, RC car).
    Ask: "Among near-paraphrases, is this the tightest conventional frame (e.g. app idea ≫ app concept; email body ≫ body of the email)?"
    Reject padded or truncated variants of a better-locked frame.
    Examples: off-duty cops, sci-fi series, mountain peaks, abstract artist, death metal band, cat person
3. Formula: A fixed conversational habit — a pre-packaged social/speech reflex, the kind of thing written in quotes as something someone said.
    Includes multi-word self-contained replies, reactions, and short first-person clauses (I'm set, I fold, ain't I?, up top),
      discourse markers (as an aside, by the way, that said), and colloquial, slangy, or sarcastic habits.
    Ask: "Is this exact string itself the preferred locked reflex, or just one fill of an open pattern / a padded version of a shorter habit?"
      Open pattern or padding → not Formula.
4. Partial: A fragment that only works with a companion word.
    Includes words mainly used bound to a companion word (sippy → sippy cup, ahold → get ahold, Kart → Mario Kart, het → het up,
      peters → peters out, utero → in utero), clippings used only inside a compound (res → hi-res, pedi → mani-pedi),
      and fragments missing a needed word (a snap → it's a snap, she's).
    Includes multi-word fragments of a specific fixed expression, quote, simile, title, or name (as a bone, Leo the, out on a, la carte, a part of).
      Ask: "Does this evoke one specific longer expression that completes it?" If yes → Partial; if no → Non-unit.
    If the string is commonly used on its own → Concept (lac, crawl in). Includes neither standalone shortenings (fridge) nor standard prefixes/suffixes.
5. Variant: Variant spelling that is valid but non-dominant (red up → redd up, Otelo → Otello).
6. Formulaic: Built on the fly from a productive template rather than stored as its own unit. Unrelated to Formula.
    Letter/number slot-fills in a template (plan A, side A, disc two, B-list, act III, x-axis, Psalm 25) and transparent re- verbs (retie, resift).
    If that fill has its own meaning → Concept (plan B, A-list, B-roll, A-frame).
    Never ordinary words or fixed phrases (more, you are here → Concept).
7. Non-unit: Grammatical phrase that doesn't fit in the first 4 categories and doesn't evoke a specific longer expression (are here, cat meat, eat less).
    Does not include single words, except where a guideline above says otherwise.
8. Nonsense: Everything else.
