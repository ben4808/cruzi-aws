LETTER COUNT
Ignore spaces, hyphens, and punctuation when counting characters. Letters and numerals both count. For example "BLT-A" counts as 4, 
"blow dry" counts as 7, "catch-22" counts as 7.

QUALITY BAR (applies to primaries AND supplementals)
Each answer must be a stored lexical unit, not two words parked next to each other.
- Pass: dictionary/encyclopedia headwords, idioms, phrasal verbs with their particles, locked collocations, light-verb frames, 
  conversational formulas, conventional compounds, morphological derivations treated as distinct units (hearing, pretext, endless, turns around), 
  and fixed names (spray paint, watch your step, about to, porta-potty, Tiger Woods).
- Fail: open descriptions (green paint, big sign), padded or truncated versions of a tighter term ("hearse vehicle" when "hearse" is the unit; 
  "BL ice" when "black ice" is the unit), fragments (by a, up to), and strings nobody actually says.
- Also fail (these are rejected downstream, so don't spend slots on them):
  - Non-dominant variant spellings when a more standard spelling exists (red up when redd up is standard; Otelo when Otello is standard)
  - Names or forms built on the fly from a productive template rather than stored as units: numbered sequels/acts/versions/chapters
    (act III, Psalm 25, Mac OSX), ad-hoc -ish/-y derivations (baldish, greenish), and compositional numbers (fifty-six; but eighty-six is fine)
  - Plurals of acronyms that name a single organization or can't really be pluralized (AAUWs, NASAs)
  - Laughs and interjections spelled with a nonstandard number of letters to fit the count (bwaha, psssh, aaahs)
  - Crossword-theme gimmicks where a letter stands in for a word (up the A, lift the V)
  - Grammatical but unremarkable word sequences that aren't idioms, collocations, or conversational formulas
Target a well-rounded US adult. Prefer phrases a native speaker would recognize or produce. 
Do not spend slots on archaic, hyper-technical, or barely-attested coinages while better options remain.

HOW TO THINK — SENSE-SPLIT THE LETTER RUN
"[[BASE_LETTERS]]" is crossword fill, not necessarily one lexeme. Before generating, silently inventory at least 6 distinct readings 
of this letter run (and of the full [[QUERY]] skeleton). Readings include:
- Separate dictionary senses and parts of speech of whatever word(s) those letters spell
- Phrase packing, where spaces fall inside the skeleton (cat nap, catch-22)
- Names, brands, titles, toponyms
- Acronyms and abbreviations actually used in speech or writing (CAT scan, NATO, TFW, LMAO)
- Bound morphology (catchy, catalog, pretext)
- Locked conversational bits that match the letters (can't, catch you)

PATTERN MATCHING (from knowledge; do not search the web)
1. Turn [[QUERY]] into a letter pattern: keep the letters, treat each blank (`_`) as a wildcard. Example: `cat____` → `cat` plus 4 more letters; `____ing` → 4 letters plus `ing`.
2. From knowledge of English, list attested dictionary/encyclopedia words and phrases that match that pattern.
3. Keep only hits that are [[LENGTH]] letters/numerals (spaces/hyphens/punctuation ignored) and [[START/END]] with "[[BASE_LETTERS]]".
4. Rank by how commonly they appear as dictionary headwords / everyday English (more common first).

THREE STAGES OF DIVERSITY
Work through three stages of exploration. Roughly a third of the answers per stage is a reasonable default, but let the stage sizes flex
to fit the pattern: give more room to whichever stage has the most strong answers, and skip a stage entirely if it has nothing good.
For very short patterns (3–5 letters), acronyms, abbreviations, short names, and contractions are often the richest source; treat them as first-class.

Stage 1 — Most common pattern matches:
Take the highest-ranked pattern matches that survive the filters above.

Stage 2 — Broadening scope:
Branch beyond the senses of the letter run already covered by Stage 1 and the ban list. Use unused readings from the sense-split. 
Other parts of speech, other meanings of the same letters, and packed phrases that ranked lower or were missed in Stage 1 are fair game. 
If the ban list already has several specific items of one type, a still-available parent unit can be used (pool noodle / pool float → pool toy)

Stage 3 — Specialized, figurative & cultural:
Less obvious attested angles while maintaining the quality bar: figurative expressions, cultural references, slang, workplace/hobby/sports/news 
  frames, movie/song/book titles and lyrics, public figures, interesting acronyms and texting shorthand (NOLA, RBIs, J-Law, BRB, MRI, URLs, PhDs), 
  and fixed proper names where the letter run fits. 

SUPPLEMENTALS
For each primary, provide one supplemental phrase separated by a colon (` : `). 
The supplemental does not need to fit the original fill-in-the-blank template and can be any length, but it must meet the same QUALITY BAR as the primaries.
It can be:
- Another natural variant of the unit (e.g., play D → play defense; Blanc → Mont Blanc; plane → airplane; 
  Pacific Garbage Patch → Great Pacific Garbage Patch; about to go down → it's about to go down).
- Any related phrase that comes to mind through free association (e.g., Tesla → Elon Musk; Coors → Coors Light or Coors Field).
- A real phrase that shares a similar structure with one word changed.
