TASK
Classify each provided English word or phrase into one of five buckets for Phrase Unity. 

Primary question: Is this string a conventional, reusable unit in the language? If so, which bucket does it belong to?

Do NOT score familiarity, usefulness, or regionality/dialect.

CORE RULES
- Domain-Relative: Evaluate jargon inside its own field. A technical term is a Concept within its domain even if outsiders do not know it.
- Borderline Tie-Breaker: If a string sits on the border between categories, always default to the less unified, more literal category. 
   The priority order is: Nonsense → Non-unit → Formula → Collocation → Concept.
- Single Words: Any single written word (containing no spaces or hyphens) is automatically either a Concept or Nonsense.
- Hyphenated Phrases: Phrases containing hyphens cannot be considered automatically Single Words. Evaluate them as multi-word phrases.
- Proper Names: Attempt to parse all phrases as a proper name. A proper name is a Concept—even when it includes a generic category word 
   (bar, cup, sauce, etc.), if that full string is how the specific thing is referred to.
- Strict Canonicity: The phrase must match its conventional word order and complete framing. Awkward inversions (roof synthetic underlayment), 
  truncated phrases ("count your chickens", "isn't my first rodeo" vs "this isn't my first rodeo"), or non-default naming variants 
  ("diving site" when "dive site" is the standard name) break Concept status. These must be demoted to Non-unit.
- Simple Inflections: If the citation/base form would be a Concept, simple grammatical inflections of that same unit remain Concepts.
   Tense, person, number, gerund, and participle changes do not demote a Concept to Collocation or Non-unit.
- Gestalt/cultural baggage: If the phrase evokes a unified cultural script and causes the mind to make many associations beyond the literal
   meaning, classify it as Concept as long as it remains a canonical unit.
- For Collocation vs. Concept, use the Substitution Test. If you change a modifier (e.g., 'light dinner' to 'small dinner' or 'heavy rain' to 'intense rain') 
   and the phrase loses a specific, culturally understood slot or function, the original is a Concept.
- Look at the full range of a phrase's polysemy (multiple meanings) in order to catch potential non-literal meanings. Especially consider
   possible meanings in Internet slang.

CLASSIFICATION BUCKETS
1. Concept — Semantic Fusion / Established Name
The string functions as a locked, canonical unit for an established idea, thing, idiom, product, or title.
Includes: Technical terms, idioms, proper names, acronyms, and distinct multi-word ideas.
Examples: climate change, screwdriver, ATM, cell phone, parallel parking, bit the bullet, making sense, Reese's Peanut Butter Cup, bag of chips, stuff

2. Collocation — Type-Level Statistical Habit
The words mean exactly what they say and are paired together often. It feel like a real phrase in practice and in the language. 
Includes: Highly frequent modifier–noun or noun–noun pairings that describe a general type of thing.
Examples: private parking, song release, chip bag, heavy rain, strong coffee

3. Formula — Social Script
A literal phrase or sentence used as a conversational reflex, routine, or social script. The literal meaning of the words remains intact, 
  but the phrase is used as a pre-packaged social interaction.
Formulas must be the absolute dominant, reflex version of the social script. If a minor word substitution makes the phrase feel less like 
  a pre-packaged reflex and more like an on-the-spot sentence, default to Non-unit.
Examples: thank you, how are you?, I had fun!, I'm gonna head out, how was your day, let me know, no worries

4. Non-unit — Grammatical Free Combination
A grammatically correct string that describes something real, but is an on-the-spot description rather than a pre-packaged chunk. 
Modifiers or synonyms can be swapped in freely without destroying a recognizable phrase.
Includes: Catalog-like descriptions, random adjective–noun chains, run-on phrases that don't form a unit
Examples: digital computer screen, brown leather wallet, how to fix a zipper, tall green tree

5. Nonsense — Broken / Incoherent String
The string has no semantic cohesion as a single idea, is grammatically broken, or is ruined by typos.
Examples: quickly wallet window, the blue desk tomorrow, baking sdoa
