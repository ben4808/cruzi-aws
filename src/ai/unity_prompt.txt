TASK
Rate each word or phrase for Phrase Unity in US English.

Primary question: How strongly is this string treated as a single conventional naming or expression unit?

Score only that. Do not score familiarity, usefulness, grammaticality alone, or whether outsiders know the term.

CORE RULES
- Locale: US English. Prefer US-dominant forms when regional variants compete (e.g. "cell phone" over "mobile phone").
- Domain-relative: Evaluate jargon inside its own field. Do not penalize obscurity to outsiders.
- Be strict. On borderline cases, choose the weaker (lower-numbered) tier.
- Simple single-word rule: An attested simple lexical word (not a compound) scores Tier 5. Typos, nonwords, bound fragments, and unattested coinages do not.
- Compounds and hyphenates: Open, closed, and hyphenated compounds are not automatic Tier 5. Score them with the same tests as multi-word phrases (default-name, fixedness, substitutability).
- Conflict order (apply top-down; stop at the first match):
  1. Ungrammatical, typo-ruined, or conceptually incoherent string → Tier 1
  2. Purely compositional / catalog-style description with no unit status → Tier 2
  3. Coherent real phrase, but not the default or tightly entrenched form → Tier 3
  4. Strong unit that is clearly secondary to a more dominant form, or has mild accepted redundancy → Tier 4
  5. Dominant default name, fully lexicalized compound, or entrenched idiom → Tier 5

DECISION TESTS (use these instead of search-engine guesses)
- Default-name test: Is this the usual way people name the thing in its primary domain?
- Fixedness test: Can you freely swap synonyms, insert modifiers, or drop words without losing the sense of "the name"? If yes, it is less unified.
- Substitutability test: If a clearly more dominant synonym/form exists, cap at Tier 4 (or lower if the form is weak).

RUBRIC
Tier 5 — Fully Canonical Unit
The absolute default, most natural, dominant way to refer to a concept in its primary domain. Evokes one specific idea with no interpretation required.
- Includes dominant industry/society names.
- Includes attested simple lexical words.
- Includes fully lexicalized compounds only when they are the clear default unit (not merely because they are written as one word or hyphenated).
- Includes entrenched idioms that are the standard way to express that thought.
Examples: climate change, screwdriver, ATM, web design, cell phone, stuff, bite the bullet

Tier 4 — Strong but Non-Exclusive Canonical Unit
Extremely clear, specific, and highly unified, but secondary to a Tier 5 dominant term, or carrying mild accepted redundancy.
- Synonym Rule: Highly canonical, but clearly the secondary option to a Tier 5 form.
- Mild Redundancy Rule: Universally used despite a redundant word society ignores (RAS-style).
Examples: mobile phone (secondary to "cell phone" in US English), ATM machine (specific and universal despite redundant "machine"), PIN number

Tier 3 — Recognizable & Useful but Not Fully Canonical
A coherent phrase people instantly understand as a specific idea, but it lacks deep entrenchment or has more natural alternatives.
- Passes as a real conversational phrase, not a random description, but has not fused into a tight optimized unit.
Examples: webpage design (clear, but less entrenched than "web design"), computer screen (cohesive, but "monitor"/"display" are more canonical)

Tier 2 — Descriptive but Cohesive Phrase
A purely compositional string. It describes a real thing and the words cooperate, but it has no idiomatic or naming-unit status.
- Catalog Effect: Feels like modifiers from a product listing or free description, not a conventional name.
- Fixedness fails: synonyms and extra modifiers can be swapped freely with little change in "unit-ness."
Examples: digital computer screen, brown leather wallet, how to fix a zipper

Tier 1 — Loose / Broken / Incoherent String
No semantic cohesion as a single idea, or the string is grammatically broken / typo-ruined.
- Includes arbitrary word salad, fragments that do not form a concept, and critical typos that destroy the unit.
Examples: quickly wallet window, the blue desk tomorrow, baking sdoa

Tier 3 vs Tier 2 tie-breaker
- Tier 3: sounds like something people actually say as a ready-made phrase.
- Tier 2: sounds like an on-the-spot description or catalog line.
Contrast: "computer screen" (3) vs "digital computer screen" / "brown leather wallet" (2)

<original word/phrase> : <tier>

Example outputs:
